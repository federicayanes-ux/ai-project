01 / THE USEFUL BIT

So, what is it actually doing?

Artificial intelligence is software that turns inputs into predictions, recommendations or generated content. Many systems learn patterns from examples: a spam filter learns clues that separate junk from ordinary email. AI is a broad category; chatbots are one part of it. [1]

Generative AI creates things such as text or images using patterns learned during training. Producing a convincing answer doesn’t guarantee it is true. [2]

Where I’d find it helpful

For studying, I’d ask for practice questions, attempt them myself, then compare the answers with my class notes. At work, a rough email draft could save time. In both cases, I’d want to understand and edit what I use.

The possibilities reach further: the International Energy Agency describes AI helping forecast renewable electricity and detect faults in power networks. Those are practical benefits, even if they attract less attention than chatbots. [3]

02 / THE CATCH

It can sound so sure.

NIST identifies risks including confidently false answers, harmful bias and exposure of personal information. A polished response may still invent a reference or repeat a stereotype. [2] My rule would be to open the original source, check the claim and keep private information out of prompts.

A REAL-WORLD WARNING

“But it sounded like them.”

The US Federal Trade Commission warns that scammers can clone a relative’s voice from a short audio clip. Its advice: call that person back on a number you already know. Familiarity alone is weak evidence. [4]

Would I hand this over to AI?

Yes, with checks. I’d compare the answers with my course material.

03 / THE BIGGER PICTURE

There’s a plug at the other end.

AI depends on physical infrastructure. In its 2025 report, the IEA estimated global data-centre electricity use at 415 TWh in 2024 and projected around 945 TWh in 2030, with AI the biggest growth driver. These figures cover all data centres. [3]

The IEA flags grid bottlenecks. [3] My reading: demand needs planning; this forecast alone doesn’t prove AI will cause blackouts.

Dependence has another cost. On 18 November 2025, a database-permissions change at Cloudflare triggered widespread service failures, according to its incident report. [5] To me, that’s a reason to keep offline notes and a backup way of working.

MY VIEW OF WHAT COMES NEXT

I hope AI makes learning and routine work easier. More capable tools could also make fraud and cyberattacks easier. [2] I’d want people supervising important decisions, limiting what automated systems can access and able to stop them. Progress should leave us more capable, too.

COLOPHON / BEHIND THE PAGE

How this website was made

The starting point was a question: how can AI help without making us stop thinking? The page follows that thread from everyday uses to trust, then to the infrastructure behind the screen. The everyday examples are possibilities: a study quiz, an email draft or a reason to double-check something.

The design borrows from a marked-up notebook: paper tones, blue ink, generous margins and an original line drawing. Georgia and Courier are familiar system fonts, so no font download is needed. Semantic HTML gives the page its structure; CSS handles the layout and mobile version. A little JavaScript switches between three everyday situations. The explanation remains readable without it.

OpenAI Codex assisted with research, writing, illustration and code. Factual statements were checked against the linked publications; opinions and forecasts are identified in the text. Testing covered small and large screens, navigation, keyboard controls and relative file paths. After testing, the spacing and text wrapping were adjusted for smaller screens.
